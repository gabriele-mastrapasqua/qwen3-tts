# Leading silence before speech — a measured half-second of perceived latency

**Status.** Observation with data, cause NOT established. No code written. The discriminating
test is short and is described in section 4.

## 1. WHAT WAS MEASURED

While comparing two checkpoints of different sizes on the streaming path, an
energy-envelope pass over every saved WAV (20 ms frames, silence floor at −30 dB of each
file's own peak) found a large and systematic difference in **lead-in silence** — the gap
between the start of the audio and the first voiced frame.

| checkpoint class | files | lead-in, median |
|---|---|---|
| 1.7B-class | 36 | **0.50 s** |
| 0.6B-class | 52 | **0.06 s** |

Per text class (median seconds of lead-in):

| class | 1.7B-class | 0.6B-class |
|---|---|---|
| short | 0.72 | 0.06 |
| conversational | 0.90 | 0.54 |
| medium | 0.50 | 0.06 |
| phonetic | 0.50 | 0.06 |
| long | 0.94 | 0.06 |

## 2. WHY IT MATTERS MORE THAN IT LOOKS

What a caller experiences is not time-to-first-audio, it is **time to the first word**:

| | TTFA p50 at the recommended point | + lead-in | first word heard |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.7B-class | 241 ms | +500 ms | **~740 ms** |
| 0.6B-class | 186 ms | +60 ms | **~250 ms** |

The engineering figures say the small checkpoint is ~24 % quicker to first audio. To an ear
it is closer to **three times** quicker to first word. Half a second of dead air at the start
of every reply is exactly what makes a voice assistant feel slow, and no amount of RTF work
addresses it: the decoder ladder (C12-WIN-10/11/12) delivered nothing at all, and this is
larger than anything that ladder was chasing.

## 3. WHAT IS NOT KNOWN

**Whether the silence is generated by the model or introduced by the serving path.** These are
different faults with different fixes and the data here cannot separate them:

* **model-side** — the checkpoint emits silent frames before speech, learned from training
  audio that carried lead-in. Fix belongs downstream: trim, or retrain.
* **engine-side** — prompt construction, speaker conditioning or the first decode quantum
  produce silent frames regardless of checkpoint. Fix belongs in the engine and would apply
  to every model.

The second is plausible and must not be assumed away: the difference tracks checkpoint SIZE
here, but size and training corpus are confounded — the two checkpoints differ in both, so
size alone is not established as the cause.

## 4. THE DISCRIMINATING TEST (short)

Run the same energy-envelope measurement on the **open** models already in the repository —
1.7B CustomVoice with a preset voice, and 0.6B — same bank, same settings, CLI and server.

* open 1.7B also shows ~0.5 s and open 0.6B does not → the effect follows model size/family,
  and it is a **model property**; the engine fix is a trim.
* both open models show ~0.06 s → the lead-in belongs to those particular checkpoints, not
  to the engine or the family.
* both open models show ~0.5 s → it is the **engine**, and every model pays it today.

Ten minutes on any box, no cloud needed for the CLI arm.

## 5. IF A TRIM IS THE ANSWER, WHAT IT MUST NOT DO

A trim removes leading frames whose energy is below a floor, before the first chunk is sent.
It changes **when** audio arrives, never **what** arrives, and it must be:

* opt-in behind a flag and default-off until qualified — it is product-visible output;
* bounded (never trim more than N ms) so a genuine soft onset is not clipped;
* measured on the real metric, `safe_play_start`, not on TTFA — trimming moves the first
  chunk earlier but a player still needs its prebuffer;
* checked against the streaming decoder's own continuity contract, since the first chunk
  seeds the decoder tail.

## 6. A SECOND FINDING FROM THE SAME PASS, RECORDED SO IT IS NOT RE-DERIVED

The duration difference between the two checkpoints on long text is **not** a slower speaking
rate. Speech time is nearly identical (13.30 s vs 13.42 s on the long class, +0.9 %); the
extra ~2 s is **pauses**: 7 inner pauses totalling 3.72 s versus 9 totalling 5.40 s. Speech
share rises with text length on the larger checkpoint (60 % → 71 %) and falls on the smaller
one (67 % → 56 % on medium). The smaller Talker keeps articulation and loses long-range
phrase planning. `phonetic` — digits and names, little to plan — is the one class where the
smaller checkpoint is not worse.
